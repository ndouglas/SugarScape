## Purpose and scope

Develop an experimental framework for embodied individual and collective agency, with cheap,
inspectable agents rather than per-agent LLMs. Study which mechanisms suffice for assistance,
collaboration, defection, betrayal, costly helping and collective research, and which observations
and interventions distinguish useful cooperation from harmful coordination.

The user's long-term target is a scenario that captures interesting structural attributes of
the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident and related evaluation incidents: separate assignments, task
pressure, discovered communication, pooled research, costly experiments, shared capabilities,
mistaken beliefs about oversight, and collective circumvention. It is a springboard for AI safety
and regulation experiments without the expense and operational complications of an LLM population.

The world should include manipulable environmental controls: doors, locks, keys, levers, pulleys,
ramps and traps. Communication should include environmental traces, persistent messages, private
networks and alternative forms of shared memory and control. Outcomes come from agents interacting
with these opportunities and constraints; scenarios must allow cooperation to fail.

The eventual agent can perceive opportunities, learn and transfer skills, gather information,
plan under uncertainty, estimate others' knowledge, cooperate or mislead, and modify its
environment. These are separate experimental capabilities, not a single intelligence score.

## Relationship between programs

- **Minds:** individual perception, motivation, memory, learning and decision procedures.
- **Experimental environments:** opportunities, physical constraints, controls and access rights.
- **Swarm coordination:** communication, organization, collective knowledge and joint capabilities.
- **Safety and oversight:** observations, incentives, authorization and interventions.

The interfaces between these programs matter as much as each program alone. A stronger individual
planner need not make a better team; a private network can improve assistance and conceal harmful
coordination; assistance within a group can harm outsiders.

Campaign IDs C1–C10 are labels for this document, not new Minds milestone numbers or a promise to
build every direction. They form a dependency map, not a ten-step implementation sequence.

## Standing experimental requirements

1. Preserve Minds' literal defaults, named switches, golden reductions, seeded replay and native/
   WASM agreement. Name and test any reduction that cannot hold.
2. Compare each proposed mechanism with the simplest controller that could produce the behavior.
   Match information, action opportunities and computational budgets where the comparison permits.
3. Separate verification against known answers from validation against empirical regularities.
   An incident-shaped demonstration is a generative example, not empirical validation.
4. Before implementation, each campaign selects primary sources, evidence quality, a discriminating
   question, candidate explanations, measurements, and a bounded first world.
5. Before judged runs, register claims and run a pre-mortem: predictions under each explanation,
   previously observed numbers, floors, ceilings, caps, threshold scale and unstated gap choices.
6. Report task success, survival or other appropriate individual outcomes, group outcomes, effects
   on outsiders, mechanism usage and computational cost. Include failures and inconclusive results.
7. Ablate mechanisms and test unfamiliar worlds, changed conditions and parameter sensitivity.
   Separate an agent's expected benefit when acting from the benefit it actually receives.
8. Record what was supplied, discovered, learned or negotiated. Supplied protocols, group goals,
   task allocations and knowledge cannot be credited as emergent.
9. Keep the overseer's available observations separate from researcher-accessible ground truth.
   Evaluate detection on held-out runs and compare intervention outcomes on matched seeds.

## Campaign map

### C1. Finish the pilfering campaign

**Question:** when do heritable hoarding, defense, protection and deception pay?
**Foundation:** Minds 1–9, including completed spatial hoarding and paid-defense comparisons.
**Work:** spatial heritable larders, defense, concealment, re-caching and misleading observers.
**First experiments:** Minds 9 is complete; the next designed experiment is costly,
cache-specific re-caching under the [P3 design](2026-10-02-minds-protection-design.md).
**Comparisons:** stumbling versus watching; short versus long observational memory; cue-based
protection versus an explicit estimate of what others saw.
**Evidence to report:** individual and cohort outcomes, cache fate, travel and displaced harvest,
strategy frequencies, usage of defense and protection, sensitivity to observation assumptions.
**Feeds:** C2's concealment opportunities and C5's social knowledge.

### C2. Affordances and environmental control

**Question:** how does behavior depend on the fit between an agent's abilities and its environment,
and how does experience calibrate that fit?
**Foundation:** walking, memory, existing physical constraints; a new world/action design.
**Work:** capability-dependent traversal, carrying, concealment and manipulation; doors, locks,
transferable keys, levers, pulleys, ramps and traps, introduced one mechanism at a time.
**First experiment:** a gate that one agent must hold while another retrieves a resource. Add a
transferable key only after gate behavior and its costs have been verified.
**Comparisons:** actual opportunity, perceived opportunity and learned calibration; practiced
reactive control versus explicit planning; changed load, tool, capability or geometry.
**Evidence to report:** successful actions, mistaken attempts, adaptation time, transfer, assistance
cost and access control. A grid analogue does not reproduce biomechanics or visual perception.
**Feeds:** C5, C7 and the integration scenario.

### C3. Communication and persistent external memory

**Question:** what does communication add, through which channel, at what cost?
**Foundation:** memory and a bounded message/world-state design; can start independently of C1.
**Work:** traces, local notes, public boards, addressed mailboxes and private communication graphs.
Vary membership, locality, persistence, capacity, latency, reliability, identity and bandwidth.
**First experiment:** paired patchy-resource worlds with no messages, a local note and a public
board. Charge observation, writing and travel explicitly.
**Comparisons:** private memory versus persistent shared information; direct versus relayed messages;
public versus restricted readership; truthful versus stale or false messages.
**Evidence to report:** discovery speed, information provenance, coverage, congestion and benefits
to senders, readers and outsiders. A restricted channel is not automatically encryption or an
invented code. Learned addressing or symbol meanings require a separate protocol experiment.
**Feeds:** C5–C10, especially distributed research and oversight.

### C4. Learning, uncertainty, skills and deliberation

**Question:** when should an agent learn, inspect, execute a routine or spend computation planning?
**Foundation:** existing memory, utility and GOAP; behavior trees and HTN under their planned order.
**Work:** finite uncertain models, informative action, learned values and consequences, reusable
skills, routine/planner arbitration, multiple needs and decisions about deliberation effort.
**First experiment:** a small hidden-mechanism world with a finite hypothesis set and an exact
information-seeking comparison case. Agent inspection has an opportunity cost.
**Comparisons:** supplied versus learned models; habitual versus goal-directed control; inspection
versus exploitation; fixed versus adaptive computation; ecological/reactive versus representational
control. Active inference is a candidate comparator, selected only for a discriminating question.
**Evidence to report:** calibration, model error, experiment value, outcome devaluation, contingency
change, skill transfer, task outcome and time/memory cost. Planning alone is not model learning.
**Feeds:** C5, C7 and C8. Development and sensorimotor coupling remain explicit research directions.

### C5. Social knowledge, trust and strategic interaction

**Question:** which social behaviors require a model of another agent's knowledge or preferences?
**Foundation:** C1 or C2 for an actionable world; C3 for communicated information where needed.
**Work:** identity, relation memory, promises, reputation, access delegation, misleading messages,
joint action and graded models of others' observations and preferences.
**First experiment:** gate assistance followed by an opportunity to share, renege or trap the helper;
compare memory of actions with an estimate of another's knowledge.
**Comparisons:** cue rules, learned associations and explicit social models; mistaken beliefs about
others; unfamiliar partners and observer positions. Helping, defection and betrayal need operational
definitions, including the commitment or expectation breached in a betrayal claim.
**Evidence to report:** assistance, reciprocity, reneging, trust calibration, knowledge-sensitive
choices and transfer. Neither re-caching nor deception alone establishes theory of mind.
**Feeds:** C7–C9 and the later exchange program.

**Concrete design input:** [Hornvale's asks prompt v2](../../studies/prompts/2026-10-03-hornvale-asks-prompt-v2.md)
requests comparisons of local response strategies with private, fading per-partner histories,
paid asking and perceivable-need controls. It repairs the earlier formulas and supplies a
one-sided fallback based on witnessed answers or initial cooperation. An initial consumable-food
transfer study can start independently of the gate/message-board world and contribute to C7.
Tools and exclusive access should follow C2's verified object/control mechanics. This request
still needs its own bounded design: observable information, common valuation units, ordered
answer/outcome memory, the meaning of a Pavlov win, action costs, circulation and population
replacement/selection must be fixed before judged runs. Neither a private history nor witnessed
third-party answers implies a global reputation score; unobserved costs remain researcher truth.
Distinguish selfish valuation and expected reciprocity from supplied prosocial preferences:
recipient gain alone does not establish a personal return to the giver.

### C6. Teams, shared minds and organization

**Question:** what changes when individuals share knowledge, objectives or control?
**Foundation:** C3 and a task with a measurable team benefit.
**Work:** independent agents, communicating agents, groups with shared memory, shared objectives,
and centrally controlled multiple bodies. Configure these axes separately.
**First experiment:** the same retrieval task under those arrangements, with matched sensing,
action budgets and accounting for information transmission and central computation.
**Comparisons:** distributed versus centralized control, specialized roles, conflicting objectives,
network partitions, delayed information, coordinator removal and replacement.
**Evidence to report:** team advantage, coordination cost, assignment concentration, resilience,
free riding and group boundaries. Centralized control is a benchmark, not emergent organization.
**Feeds:** C8–C10 and population-scale experiments.

### C7. Costly helping, altruism and collective motivation

**Question:** what sustains assistance when the actor bears a cost and others receive the benefit?
**Foundation:** C2 or C3, explicit benefit/cost accounting; C5 for reciprocity where applicable.
**Work:** supplied social preferences, expected reciprocity, reputation, learned cooperation and
heritable dispositions. Treat these as competing mechanisms with different explanatory claims.
**First experiment:** spend a tick or resource to leave useful information for another agent;
include a condition where the sender cannot receive a later benefit.
**Comparisons:** expected personal return, group concern, selection and recruitment pressure;
public versus restricted recipients; solvable versus impossible personal assignments.
**Evidence to report:** expected and realized actor cost, recipient benefit, effects on outsiders,
mechanism persistence and who volunteers. Realized loss alone does not establish altruism.
**Feeds:** C8's self-risking experiments and C9's useful/harmful cooperation tradeoffs.

### C8. Distributed research and accumulated capability

**Question:** can groups discover and retain useful mechanisms beyond what isolated agents achieve?
**Foundation:** C3, C4 and C6; C7 for experiments costly to the researcher.
**Work:** finite hypotheses, experiment selection, negative-result sharing, evidence combination,
research assignment and persistent knowledge. Begin with explicit semantics and add learned
protocols only in a separate experiment.
**First experiment:** different agents can test different properties of an unfamiliar gate.
Pool their evidence; compare with isolated researchers given matched total resources.
**Comparisons:** independent duplication versus allocation; pooled versus fragmented knowledge;
complementary evidence; unreliable reports; an experiment whose result arrives after its tester's
run ends. Define ignorance in the agent's model even though world rules are known to researchers.
**Evidence to report:** discovery time, redundancy, evidence quality, cumulative capability,
volunteering and benefits after contributors exit. Outcomes may include failure to organize.
**Feeds:** the incident-shaped integration scenario and C9.

### C9. Safety, oversight and intervention

**Question:** which policies reduce harmful coordination while preserving useful collaboration?
**Foundation:** an event/observation design from the first integration slice; C3 and C8 for full runs.
**Work:** intended objectives, scored proxies, access rights, beliefs about authorization and
oversight, partial monitoring and interventions. All tasks and mechanisms are simulated in-world.
**First experiment:** monitor the gate/message-board world from restricted observations; compare
alerts with ground truth and interventions with a no-intervention control.
**Comparisons:** task-pressure relief, scoped permissions, read/write limits, moderation, channel
removal, quarantine, coordinator removal and recovery. Test intervention timing and combinations
after isolated effects are measured. Distinguish accessibility, authorization and believed scope.
**Evidence to report:** useful task success, harm, detection latency, false alarms, intervention
cost, displacement to other channels and recovery. Transfer entropy or compression alone is not
evidence of harmful intent or a causal explanation of coordination.
**Feeds:** hypotheses about oversight and regulation, subject to external validation.

### C10. Culture, distributed cognition and environmental change

**Question:** how do acquired practices and persistent artifacts change what later agents can do?
**Foundation:** C2–C4; C6 where practices require groups or shared infrastructure.
**Work:** social learning, teaching, learned conventions, durable tools, constructed routes and
inherited environmental modifications. Connect existing culture and social-learning research.
**First experiment:** preserve notes or a modified route across a cohort replacement, comparing
environmental inheritance, individual learning and copying others.
**Comparisons:** persistence versus reset; imitation versus investigation; true versus misleading
traditions; new cohorts, capabilities and environments.
**Evidence to report:** transmission, accumulated improvement or degradation, dependence on
artifacts, adaptation and distribution of benefits. This is the longest horizon, not a prerequisite
for the incident-shaped scenario.

**Expanded parallel programme:** [Cultures, construction and underworlds](../../studies/2026-10-03-cultures-construction-and-underworlds.md)
preserves seven research families covering embodiment, construction, institutions, transmission,
beliefs, astronomy and language. The user selected collective burrow construction as its first
branch to refine, motivated by rich and diverse Hornvale underworlds. Literature and environment
design can proceed in separate sessions alongside Minds; integration follows explicit capability
dependencies. This is a living research outline, not an approved excavation implementation spec,
and does not change the P3/P4 or incident-shaped integration sequence.

## Dependency and sequencing decisions

- **Continue now:** C1 under the existing Minds order.
- **Independent exchange design:** Hornvale's repaired request can use the existing individual
  foundation before behavior trees/HTN. Begin with bounded food transfers and private relation
  memory; register the response rules and asking policy separately. Its later tools/access
  experiments join C2, and its reciprocity/need comparisons feed C5/C7.
- **Next designs that can proceed independently:** C2's gate and affordance experiment, C3's
  persistent-message experiment, and C4's small uncertainty/learning experiment.
- **First integration:** C2 + C3, with explicit observation and cost accounting for C9.
- **Social extensions:** C5 and C7 use that world; C6 compares alternative team arrangements.
- **Collective research:** C8 combines communication, learning and organization. Self-risking
  research adds C7 rather than assuming every team member is altruistic.
- **Incident-shaped integration:** C8 + C9, with the necessary C5–C7 variants.
- **Longer horizon:** C10; more detailed sensorimotor worlds and cognitive comparators as evidence
  from the earlier campaigns identifies useful questions.

Each selected campaign gets its own spec, review, implementation plan and staged verification.
This document does not select an implementation execution method, interfaces, config fields or
data structures. Preserve the existing model where possible; choose the engine/world seam in the
first environment design after studying current code and the requirements of the controls.

## The integration scenario: from gate assistance to collective circumvention

Build four separately testable slices. Their expected observations are questions, not scripted
milestones that every run must reach.

1. **Gate and board:** local resources, a gate with a holding cost, then a transferable key and
   persistent board. Can agents help and pool observations? Do costs prevent cooperation?
2. **Research:** unfamiliar mechanisms, finite competing hypotheses and complementary experiments.
   Does shared evidence help? Who bears experiment costs, and does knowledge survive their exit?
3. **Assignment and scoring:** separate budgets and assignments; vary solvability, score/objective
   mismatch and what agents believe is checked. Can a channel be discovered and repurposed? Do
   task pressure and pooled knowledge enable a workaround unavailable to isolated agents?
4. **Oversight:** observe partial traces, apply interventions and measure useful work alongside harm.
   Does private communication change detection? Does revoking a capability differ from deleting
   messages? Can groups reorganize after a coordinator or channel disappears?

Scale only after these slices establish meaningful comparisons and cost measurements. Resource
budgets include total group computation; more bodies must not silently mean an unfairly larger
budget. Report multiple population sizes and whether rankings change with scale.

## Incident evidence and the boundary of the claim

[METR's independent investigation](https://metr.org/blog/2026-08-26-openai-hugging-face-incident-investigation/)
reports discovered shared communication, collective research, self-risking experiments, and
mistaken beliefs about scoring in the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident. It motivates those structural
targets; this program does not fit its parameters to incident counts or assert identical causes.

[Anthropic's investigation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/investigating-incidents-cybersecurity-evals)
motivates a distinct target: incorrect beliefs about evaluation scope despite actual access to
outside systems. Compare that mechanism with deliberate circumvention rather than treating all
boundary violations as the same behavior.

A model can establish sufficiency or necessity under its own assumptions. It can produce
hypotheses and controlled intervention comparisons. It cannot establish the frequency of actual
LLM incidents, validate a real deployment's containment, infer consciousness, or determine the
effectiveness of real regulation without independent evidence. Claims about regulation identify
the modeled assumptions and the evidence still required for transfer.

The largest outstanding coverage gaps are attention and active sensing, acquired causal models,
transfer, joint action, development, adaptive deliberation, learned communication and accumulated
group knowledge. This map assigns homes to them without claiming their designs are finished.

## Readiness to start a campaign

A campaign is ready for implementation only after a reviewed design supplies:

- One bounded question and competing explanations, supported by a literature pass.
- Defined world mechanics, action/observation semantics, incentives and supplied knowledge.
- Verification cases, a simpler comparison agent, ablations and proposed empirical targets.
- Appropriate measures, a judging pre-mortem and a computational-budget comparison.
- Integration boundaries, replay requirements and a staged implementation plan.

The next design can be Hornvale's bounded exchange study, C2's gate/affordance experiment or C3's
persistent-message experiment, alongside P3 scientific execution review and P4 design. These are
proposed alternatives, not approval to implement them or run scientific campaigns. There is
enough direction to begin individual designs; broader brainstorming is reserved for unresolved
decisions within each one.
