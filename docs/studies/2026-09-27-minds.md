## Principle

A decision engine is an instrument. Before any social result rests on one, it has to pass two
tests:

- **Verification:** it gives the known right answer where one exists.
- **Validation:** it produces, or fails to produce, an attested behavioral regularity, with the
  failure reported as carefully as the success.

Most of the validation targets come from foraging theory, because it has what social theory mostly
lacks: quantitative, replicated predictions about where individuals go and when they leave.

## Method

- **One atom at a time.** Each engine is a milestone with its sources, its oracle, its target,
  presets, sweeps and survey claims, like the reproductions.
- **Every engine reduces to rule M.** Configured to see only what rule M sees and to value only
  what rule M values, each engine must reproduce rule M's fingerprints on every golden Sugarscape
  preset. This is the program's standing check that its engines agree with each other. Where an
  engine can't reduce (a planner whose search order draws differently), the difference is named
  and tested.
- **Literal default, named switch.** The decision rule is a switch on the Sugarscape config.
  `book` stays the default, and every golden entry stays unedited.
- **Findings are the point.** Where the book's rule, or an engine, fails a regularity, the failure
  is the result. Say which part of the regularity's assumptions it breaks.
- **Deterministic and cheap.** Seeded, replayable, native and WASM agreeing. Cost per agent per
  tick will be reported for each engine, starting with Minds 2's baseline for the book rule and
  the utility mind.

Then:

- **Compositions:** utility picks the goal, GOAP plans it, A* prices the moves.
- **Harder scenarios:** tools, exclusive sites, carrying, seasons.
- **Scaling:** agents × planning horizon × world size, and where each engine stops being affordable.

## Where the social programs join

- **Exchange** (asking and answering between selfish agents) needs individuals who can tell each
  other apart (Sugarscape's ids), memory of each relation that fades (Minds 3), and the holder's
  cost of giving over a horizon (foresight welfare, already in the engine). It needs no planner,
  so it can start now that Minds 3 is done.
- **Swarm coordination** needs walking (Minds 2), memory (Minds 3) and a planner (Minds 4).
- **Deception, betrayal and scheming** need false signals, promises, reputation and models of other
  minds. It's written up only once exchange and swarm results show which of these are needed.

## What's already known

### Minds 1

From Minds 1's survey (20 seeds, tick 1000; two cone patches at input ratios 1 to 4.42, 100 agents
of metabolism 1). *s* is the matching exponent (1 is input matching, below 1 undermatching), fitted
per seed from means of log ratios (Earn and Johnstone). Full results are in the README's Minds 1
section.

- **The utility mind reduces to rule M.** With travel 0, crowding 0 and idle `stay`, every
  book-rule Sugarscape preset without combat gives rule M's fingerprint.
- **Rule M undermatches, and input matching fails.** *s* has median 0.72 at vision 1–6, 0.63 at
  5–10 and 0.90 at 10–20. Every seed undermatches at 5–10 and 10–20, and 17 of 20 at 1–6.
- **"Free" fails before "ideal" does.** Rule M keeps an agent in place when nothing it sees is
  better. With nobody starving, a median 66 of 100 never reach a patch. Survival plays no part:
  *s* is identical seed by seed with and without starvation.
- **Wandering frees the agents but not the split.** Under idle `wander` a median 0.5 % are off
  patch, but *s* falls from 0.72 to 0.40. The poorer patch is overfilled (41 agents on 36 sugar a
  tick), so the split likely follows where the wanderers arrive. That cause is not measured.
- **Catchment is close on the median but fails as judged:** predicted *s* 0.709, median 0.722, but
  only 6 of 20 seeds within 0.1.
- **Crowding and travel act only when an agent sees both patches** (vision 10–20; at 1–6 they leave
  the split unchanged). Crowding m = 1 raises *s* from 0.90 to 0.95, and Sutherland's matching
  holds, against our expectation. Travel k = 0.5 lowers *s* to 0.73, against Baum and Kraft's
  direction; their travel was a cost of switching, ours a preference for nearby sugar.

**A suggestion for Minds 2 and 3:** rule M's failure is in "free", not "ideal". Walking (Minds 2)
and memory of sites seen (Minds 3) are what could let an agent leave a place where it sees nothing,
without wandering at random.

### Minds 2

Minds 2 adds A\*, walls and fences, and walking: the decision rule picks its target as before, and
the agent takes `speed` steps a tick along an A\* path instead of jumping. A\* matches Dijkstra on
1 324 random grids (665 walled tori and 659 octile maps of the 2 000 drawn; a grid whose start or
goal is a wall is skipped) and Sturtevant's published optimal lengths on 195 Moving AI scenarios.
At vision 1 walking is jumping, fingerprint for fingerprint. From the survey (20 seeds) unless a
sweep is named; full results are in the README's Minds 2 section.

- **The book's carrying capacity needs the jump.** On `ii-2-unit` the population (mean over ticks
  300–500) has median 181 walking against 228 jumping, lower in all 20 seeds. The book's 224
  fails. In the sweeps (an observation, not a judged claim) capacity tracks reach per tick,
  roughly min(speed, vision): walking at vision 1–6 (181.5) is about jumping at vision 1
  (182.7), walking at speed 3 (212.8) is near jumping at vision 1–3 (205.5), and walking at
  speed 6 (225.2) is near jumping at vision 1–6 (228.5).
- **Speed brings it back.** In the `walk-speed` sweep the mean rises from 181.5 at one step a tick
  to 227.5 at ten, against 228.5 jumping.
- **Walking wipes out the gain from vision.** In the `walk-vision` sweep the jump's capacity rises
  from 182.7 at vision 1 to 241.5 at 1–10; walking stays between 181.5 and 188.8.
- **Skewed wealth and seasonal migration survive walking.** Wealth skewness median 1.26 against
  1.27 jumping. 55 % of agents migrate with the seasons against 83 %.
- **Walking doesn't bring back the waves.** At tick 100 a median 0.6 % of agents are more than 25
  sites from the starting block's center (0.8 % jumping). Walking isn't the missing mechanism.
- **Baum and Kraft's travel claim fails again, in the opposite direction.** This time travel is a
  real cost of switching: a fence between the patches with one gap. Moving the gap to the far end
  lowers *s* from 0.90 (walking, no fence) to 0.85, in 18 of 20 seeds. Undermatching grows. The
  `ifd-detour` sweep compares gap offsets, not fence against no fence, so it can't back this; its
  fence arm is flat within noise, its wall arm falls about 0.016 (untested).
- **A confound in the fenced worlds.** The fences at x = 2 and x = 28 leave 25 columns on the
  richer patch's side and 33 on the poorer's, so random placement starts about 57 % of agents on
  the poorer side. Its effect isn't measured.
- **The visual barrier is Weak.** An opaque wall instead of the fence gives *s* 0.91 against 0.88;
  only 10 of 20 seeds are within 0.05.
- **At 2.10 : 1 alone the walking arms can't be told apart.** N₁/N₂ is 1.96 with the far fence,
  1.99 with the near fence and the wall, and 1.97 walking with no fence, all above the jump's
  1.88. For the far gap the ratio (1.96 against 1.97) and *s* (0.846 against 0.899) point the same
  way, but none of the walking arms' ratio differences is tested, and their medians are within
  0.03 of each other.

**Cost** (µs per agent-tick; the whole tick with all rules, release CLI, 2 000 ticks, seeds 1–5):

|---|---|---|
| `ii-2-unit` | rule M, jump (Minds 1 baseline) | 1.09 |
| `ii-2-unit` | utility mind, jump (Minds 1 baseline) | 1.13 |
| `ii-2-unit` | rule M, jump (Minds 2 run) | 1.05 |

jump on `ii-2-unit`, and about 2.9 times at vision 10–20 behind fences.

**A suggestion for Minds 3:** walking makes rule M's lack of memory costly. A walker forgets a
target as soon as it can't see it, and an agent that can't see the other patch through a wall is
exactly the case memory would change.

### Minds 3

Minds 3 adds memory and belief. A rememberer records the sites it sees; a remembered site out of
sight joins its candidates with a believed value, `recall` (what it saw) or `project` (that plus
growback since, capped at the most ever seen there). Hidden truffle spots, placed by a hash and
found only by stopping on them, give memory something only it can exploit. Memory's value is
measured as an information asymmetry: rememberers against non-rememberers in the same world, paired
seed by seed. From the survey (20 seeds, ticks 200–500) unless a sweep is named; full results are
in the README's Minds 3 section.

- **Memory mostly hurts under rule M.** Rememberers end up poorer in five of six worlds: −113 on the
  open sugarscape, −114 behind the wall (7 % of rememberers alive at tick 500 against 74 %), −48
  through the seasons, −82 among truffles and −90 with the patches out of sight. That last −90 is on
  holdings near 100 000 (under 0.1 %), and there rememberers reach a patch sooner (median tick 27.5
  against 33). They pay only on the trapline world, +69 in every seed, measured with half the
  agents remembering (share 0.5), not the preset's share 1.
- **Pricing travel removes the loss, largely by using memory less; no gain was shown.** Rule M
  prices no travel, so a far remembered site believed full beats a near one, a likely contributor to
  the loss. Pricing travel (the utility mind, travel 0.5) raises the advantage on `mem-open` in
  every seed, by a median 119, to about neutral: +7.4 (IQR −18 to +37), rememberers richer in only
  10 of 20 seeds (Weak). Meanwhile the share of rememberers' choices aimed at a remembered site
  falls from 0.68 to 0.10. Across the arms, the loss tracks how often memory is used: `project`
  uses it about twice as often as `recall` (0.68 against 0.34 on `mem-open`) and loses more. The
  same switch takes `mem-walled` from −114 to −55 (still a loss) and `mem-truffles` from −82 to
  +1.5, again with memory used less (0.96 to 0.65, and 0.73 to 0.25).
- **Projection is worse than recall,** the opposite of Hornvale's goblins: −113 against −34 on
  `mem-open`, worse in every seed, with a larger belief error (2.67 against 2.46). The belief error
  is measured only on chosen targets, the ones believed best, so it carries a selection bias, and
  isn't clean evidence either way on Hornvale's claim. Likely reason:
  Hornvale's planner prices what competitors take meanwhile; ours doesn't. `stale_choices` is about
  0.93–0.95 under both beliefs, so staleness alone doesn't separate them.
- **Truffles and traplining.** Rememberers take about 2.3 times the truffles on `mem-truffles`
  (0.0147 against 0.0065 an agent-tick) yet end poorer. On `mem-trapline` the index of return
  variability is 0.15. The non-rememberer control (share 0.5) scores 0.36, which also passes the
  0.8 threshold, so the index alone doesn't show that memory makes the trapline; the sparse map
  likely channels anyone's wanderings. The payoff holds: 0.050 truffles an agent-tick against
  0.011. Gill's competition effect fails: the median revisit interval is 50
  ticks with both 5 and 20 agents.
- **Forgetting tracking regrowth isn't shown.** Under `recall` the advantage is negative at every
  span and rate, so the "best" span is the least-harmful span. At growback 1 it sits at the lowest
  span tested (10, IQR 10–10), the floor of the grid, against 25 at 0.25. The survey's verdict is
  Weak (shorter in 12 of 20 seeds), but a best span pinned at the floor can't show forgetting
  tracking regrowth, only that memory hurts least when shortest. Under `project` the median
  advantage is highest at span 10 at every rate. Memory for everyone lowers walking's capacity further: 154 against 181.
- **Memory isn't worth more when rare.** In the `mem-share` sweep (means, untested) the advantage is
  −88, −73, −76, −86 and −98 at shares 0.1 to 0.9.
- **The marginal value theorem is untestable for these engines.** With rich patches, foragers never
  leave (0 departures in 20 seeds). With depleting patches (two redesigns, tried and withdrawn),
  they starve before switching. Likely reason: these minds compare the values of sites, not rates
  of intake, and hold no estimate of the habitat's average. The theorem moves to Minds 4.

**Cost** (µs per agent-tick, measured as in Minds 2). The machine was loaded, so Minds 2's presets
were re-timed alongside:

| Preset | Engine | µs per agent-tick |
|---|---|---|
| `mem-open` | rule M, walk, memory 100 | 19.7 |
| `mem-truffles` | rule M, walk, memory 200, truffles | 28.9 |
| `mem-mvt` | utility mind, walk, memory 400 | 14.5 |
| `mem-walled` | rule M, walk, vision 10–20, wall, memory 200 | 8.7 |
| `walk-capacity` (re-timed) | rule M, walk, speed 1 | 4.91 |

Against `walk-capacity` timed now, memory costs 4.0 times on the open sugarscape and 5.9 times
with truffles. `mem-walled` is cheaper than `ifd-fence` because its rememberers die, and
`ifd-fence`'s figure looks inflated by the load (1.9 times its Minds 2 figure, against 1.05 times
for `walk-capacity`).

**The lesson carried forward:** under these minds memory mostly misleads. Pricing travel removes
the loss, largely by using memory less; no gain was shown. Rule M's missing travel price is a
likely contributor, and projection that ignores competitors another.

### Minds 4

Minds 4 adds a planner, GOAP, and the marginal-value rule. The planner searches forward with Minds
2's A\* over a domain's states, and it finds the known optimal plan lengths on gripper (3n − 1 or
3n), two logistics families (4n) and a blocks-world family (4n − 2), and Dijkstra's cost on 500
random STRIPS instances. In the foraging domain an agent plans the fastest run of harvests that
gathers H ticks of food, pricing each walk by its length. The marginal-value rule keeps a running
mean ρ of its intake and leaves when nothing within a step is worth ρ. `memory.prior: map` gives
founders the whole map, the theorem's ideal forager. From the survey (20 seeds) unless a sweep is
named; full results are in the README's Minds 4 section.

- **What the planner considers matters more than how it plans.** The spec shortlisted the K best
  known sites by value. With the map known those are far peak centers, and the planner shuttles
  between them and starves: 4 of 60 agents alive at tick 1000 on the theorem's world, against 28
  of 60 with the shortlist ranked by value ÷ (distance + 1). The rate shortlist became the default,
  with value kept as a named switch.
- **The theorem's world, balanced as measured.** At the spec's growback 0.05 a patch regrows 1.25 a
  tick, more than one forager eats, so it never runs out. Growback became 0.02 and the population
  3, by the rule set before measuring.
- **Longer travel, longer stays.** Mean residence rises with spacing in 20 of 20 seeds under GOAP
  (18.6 to 25.7 ticks from s = 12 to 20, 22.8 at 24) and in 19 of 20 under the marginal-value rule,
  where the effect is small (11.8 to 12.9). Rule M with the same knowledge, and GOAP with the value
  shortlist, reverse it in 20 of 20.
- **Overstaying isn't shown.** As judged (the last tick's gain against the mean so far), GOAP
  overstays at 57 % of departures (Holds, 18 of 20) and the marginal-value rule at 39 % (Fails, 0
  of 20). Without the ticks in transit, GOAP is at 20–33 % and the marginal-value rule at 2–7 %, so
  GOAP's literal overstaying is likely the walk out. Under both, overstaying falls as travel grows,
  opposite to Constantino and Daw.
- **Memory pays a planner, among the living.** The rememberers' advantage under GOAP is +69 open,
  +99 among truffles and +33 behind the wall (19, 20 and 20 of 20 seeds), against rule M's −113,
  −82 and −114 and the travel-priced utility mind's +7.4, +1.5 and −55. 98–99 % of rememberers'
  plans use a remembered site out of sight. With the dead counted as 0 it's +35 (18 of 20), +26 (14
  of 20) and +10 (15 of 20), so part of it is survivorship. And the gain is relative to planners who
  don't remember: GOAP's non-rememberers are much poorer than rule M's (240 against 438 on the open
  sugarscape). A bias in plan survival that favors rememberers (a non-rememberer's off-axis target
  leaves its candidates sooner) isn't isolated.
- **Horizon and K** (sweeps, untested). Population on `ii-2-unit` walking under GOAP rises from
  143.3 at H = 2 to 206.4 at H = 10, holds at 206.3 at 20 and falls to 195.7 at 40, against 181 for
  walking rule M and 224 for the jump. Most of GOAP's decisions there are the rate fallback, so the
  gain is likely as much the rate choice as the planning. K barely matters past 4 (196.5 at 2,
  207.9 at 4, 206.4 at 8, 204.9 at 12). On `goap-truffles` the advantage is positive at every share
  remembering (+90 to +113), where rule M's is negative at every share.

### Minds 5

Minds 5 adds a carrying limit, caches an agent buries and digs, and a winter everywhere at once.
Digging is foraging: below half its reserve an agent's caches join its candidate sites, and burying
stops at the reserve, so it doesn't dig back what it just buried. Three caching rules each carry one
hypothesis's mechanism: `even` (compartment-independent), `compensate` (Amodio et al.'s weights)
and `plan` (what-where-when memory and a cycle finder, for the Future Planning Hypothesis). Raby's
and Amodio's protocols run as a world mode on a walled rig, and central-place provisioning runs
under the marginal-value rule. From the survey (20 seeds) unless stated; full results are in the
README's Minds 5 section.

- **Every rule leaves its signature in the labs.** Each rule's caches match a hand derivation in
  both protocols (tests). In Raby's protocol, compensating agents cache 26.4 ± 0.1 where breakfast
  was missing against 3.6 ± 0.1 (the birds: 16.3 ± 1.8 against 5.4 ± 1.8). Planners are Weak
  (160 of 320): looking one day ahead, a planner caches nothing when breakfast came first, because
  tomorrow has food. Even splitters tie, the expected control. In Amodio's protocol every rule's
  predicted pattern appears in 20 of 20 seeds.
- **The paper's Bayesian comparison, written from its text, reproduces its numbers:** 0.72, 0.16
  and 0.002 on its own Table 2. On our agents it picks each rule's hypothesis. The planners' 0.55
  and 0.62 are ceilings (identical agents count as one bird), not weak evidence. The jays' 0.72 for
  the compartment-independent model looks like our even splitters' signature.
- **FPH 2: the paper's Figure 6 caption slips.** It says "K1 and K2", but the paper's model
  constraints (r_K2 ≤ r_K1, r_K3 for Food-First) give K1 and K3, which our derivation matches.
- **The winter world, balanced as measured.** walk-capacity's own agents couldn't meet the spec's
  summer-surplus rule, so the world has 175 agents of metabolism 1, γ 100, β 32, a carrying limit
  of 50 and a horizon of 20 for every rule (at 10, `even` did worse than `none`). The regrowth-only
  test ignored the sugar standing when winter starts, so the balance test became empirical: `none`
  at least 20 points below `even` in the first winter.
- **Caching gets agents through winter.** First-winter survival is 49 % with no caching, 74 %
  with an even share, 74 % compensating and 88 % planning, each higher in 20 of 20 seeds. After
  five winters it's 49 %, 57 %, 55 % and 70 %. Per founding agent, alive at tick 1000: 46 %, 54 %,
  53 % and 67 %. By tick 1000, 75 % of what even splitters buried is not dug (16 % lost with the
  dead, 59 % still buried); for planners it's 56 % (12 % and 44 %).
- **Rule M heads for its biggest cache** (70–76 % of choices where the biggest isn't also a
  nearest), a mean 5.0–6.6 steps away when its nearest is 1.4–2.1. That's likely one cause of the
  deaths, not isolated.
- **The expected planner mechanism isn't seen.** Planners who survived a winter forecast a mean
  54 of winter intake and bury a median 5.4 in the second summer against 82 in the first, likely
  because the sugar standing when winter starts counts as intake from sites. In the mixed world,
  planners are 25 % of founders and 32 % of survivors at tick 200.
- **Central place: loads rise with distance** (68 to 110 per trip from 4 to 24 columns, 20 of
  20 seeds). **On the analytic patch the learned rule's loads (49, 60, 65) are at or near the
  optimum (51, 60, 65), but by cancellation.** The per-tick comparison can't see the lattice's parity, and
  with ρ fixed at the optimal rate it takes 45, 60 and 63; the learned ρ's decay cancels that.
- **Lima's equal near and far loads fail as pre-registered** (120 far against 54 near). The
  habitat put the near patch on the way to the far one. By destination, the loads are 75 and 71.
  With the far patch on the other side, they are 83 and 73, and 100 and 74 with ρ held fixed. That
  row, and the one with ρ fixed in the first habitat, lose agents: 80 and 88 of 100 alive, against
  97–99. An agent goes far only when a far site is strictly richer (ties go to the nearer). That
  selection is the likely cause, but it explains row 4's 35 % gap only in part. The distance
  effect likely rests on ρ decaying over the walk and on the carrying limit, which are reasons the
  theory doesn't use, though the fixed-ρ row without the crossing shows far loads still exceeding
  near ones (100 against 74), so ρ decay is not necessary for the gap.
- **Linear loading pulses.** A delivery lifts ρ above any site's yield, so 54 % of trips end almost
  at once and 46 % fill to the limit. Loads grow with distance anyway (121 to 151), as with
  Kacelnik and Cuthill's starlings.

### Minds 6

Minds 6 lets agents steal each other's caches, and so opens the pilfering campaign. An agent that
arrives on a site draws once for each foreign cache there and finds each with chance `find`. It
takes the first it finds, one take per arrival, and an owner digging its own cache draws nothing.
- **Owner memory** can be switched off, which is Andersson and Krebs's equal-recovery case as an
  equal chance per draw.
- **Loot** is kept, to be buried again, or eaten into a stomach that feeds the thief. The stomach
  is a spec amendment; eaten loot had starved thieves.
- **Cheaters** are dealt by id and never bury.
- **A bury cost** is Andersson and Krebs's C.
- **Every cache's fate** can be logged (when a caller asks for it, as the survey does): dug, pilfered, lost or still buried.

From the survey (20 seeds, ticks 1–200) unless stated. Full results are in the README's Minds 6

### Minds 7

Minds 7 is P1b of the pilfering campaign. Unlike Minds 1–6 it is a reproduction first: Vander Wall
and Jenkins's (2003) genetic algorithm, built as written as its own non-spatial model kind, `hoard`.
Twenty agents forage through a 100-day season of 20 bouts a day, store what they don't eat in a
larder (a burrow they can defend) with heritable probability L or else in scattered caches, guard
the larder with heritable propensity D, and pilfer each other's stores in proportion to their
apparency. Survivors breed in proportion to their leftover stores for 60 generations, with
heritability 0.8. The paper's Appendix (pp. 664–666) fixes most of the procedure. Each gap it
leaves (the defense logistic's slope, the segregation variance V_seg, what a search that lands on a
defended larder gets, the stores of the dead, and others) is filled as a stated choice in the spec's
amendments and exposed as a named switch. What Minds 6 taught comes in as switches too: owners who
must find their own scattered caches (`owner_recovery`), and Minds 6's cheater that never caches
(`cheaters`, with `cheater_fitness`). The page shows one column per agent (state, L and D, larder
up and scatter down), by-generation charts of L, D, survivors, the larder share and the two loss
rates, and the season's larder share by bout.

**Per burrow, and why it changed.** The paper can be read two ways on how a larder is found. Its
Appendix sums "all larder hoards of other individuals weighted by apparency of larder hoards"
(p.665), which can weigh every larder item; but p.662 defines app_lard as "the relative probability
of detecting a burrow of another individual while searching for food", and p.664 has searchers
encounter "burrow entrances of other individuals, with probabilities determined by the apparency
values". We first chose per item. Under it larders never took over in 0 of 15 diagnostic runs, which
prompted the change (the survey, run after it, kept per item as a full row: 0 of 1 350 runs at
ratios up to 0.45). A diagnosis found that a larder's detection grows
with its size, raids start from those hits, and larders never grow large enough to defend. On
re-reading with that in view, per burrow is the better-supported reading, and it became the
default. **The change was prompted by a result, and it is disclosed as such**: per item is kept as
the named switch, and the survey runs the claims under both readings and reports per item as a
failure to reproduce.

From the survey (50 runs a condition, seeds 1–50, each run 60 generations; the ratio grid is
app_lard 1, 2 and 3 by app_scat/app_lard 0.05–0.45 with app_scat ≤ 0.9, 1 350 runs per reading;
judges committed before any run; per burrow unless stated). Full results are in the README's Minds 7
section.

- **The threshold is Weak.** Our fit is logit = −5.98 + 25.45 × ratio (ρ² 0.50), with its 50 % point
  at 0.235 against their 0.219 (−8.00 + 36.59 × ratio, ρ² 0.61). The 50 % point is within ±0.03.
  Below 0.2, 7 of 450 runs took over, all at 0.15; above 0.3, 285 of 300 did (95 %).
- **All-or-nothing holds in the ending, not the speed (Fails as judged).** 98.6 % of runs end below
  0.2 or above 0.95, but only 10.7 % of takeovers pass 0.95 by generation 10 (median generation 15).
  Read as within 10 generations of lifting above 0.2, it is 37.7 %. Their own Fig. 2A example passes
  0.95 near generation 16; in that cell (app_lard 1, ratio 0.30) ours do at a median 16.5. V_seg
  likely sets the speed.
- **Larder loss above scatter loss: Weak (93.8 % of runs, not all).** Every miss is a takeover run
  at ratios 0.35–0.45. Medians over generations 1–10 are 90 % a day for larders and 27 % for
  scatter, against their 186 % and 24 % (context only; their rate is undefined).
- **The predictor Holds, against a weak baseline.** *Some agent's larder loses less than the mean
  scatter loss in generations 1–10* (our paraphrase of p.663) is 84.5 % accurate against 59.3 % for comparing means. But the
  ratio alone (takeover at ratio ≥ 0.25) gives 83.7 %, so the minimum isn't claimed to explain the
  outcome.
- **Scatter withstands loss:** 20.5 % a day over generations 1–10 in runs without takeover (20.8 %
  over 1–60), against their 18 % (reported).
- **The CV judge was omitted.** Amendments item 10 called for one; the judge commit left it
  reported, before any run, and none was added after. The ratio is 2.85 within runs (1.49 across
  runs) against their 1.73: "almost twice" is not matched.
- **Neither reading reproduces both.** Per item reproduces the loss statistics (larder above scatter
  in 1 350 of 1 350 runs, a CV ratio of 1.75, a 210 % median larder loss) but never the outcome. Per
  burrow gets the outcome and misses those. Likely their detection or defense differs from both in
  some unstated way.
- **The reproduction is contingent on three choices.** Per burrow (chosen after per item failed), a
  defense slope of 10 and V_seg 0.5 (both fixed before any run). At app_lard 2 the 50 % point in the
  sensitivity runs is 0.236 at those values, 0.351 at V_seg 0.25, 0.174 at V_seg 1 and about
  0.09–0.14 at slope 4 (two of the three fits near-separated). Their
  sensitivity analysis "will be reported elsewhere" (p.665); three searches did not find it
  (unfound).

**New ground (reported, not judged).**

- **Visibility.** Takeover rises with app_scat at every app_lard, apart from small dips, from none
  at the lowest values (app_scat up to 0.15, 0.2 and 0.3 for app_lard 1, 2 and 3) to 78–100 % at the
  top of each row, as their untested p.663 prediction says. At a fixed app_lard
  this is the ratio axis again, and at a given ratio the three app_lard values give similar shares.
- **Owner recovery.** At ratio 0.1 nothing takes over at any recovery, and survival falls from 0.82

### Minds 8

Minds 8 is P2 of the pilfering campaign: watching. An agent who watches and sees another bury (the
site on one of the four lattice lines from it, within its vision and not behind an opaque wall)
remembers the cache for `span` ticks (7 by default), goes to it as it would to its own caches, and
on arriving takes from the first remembered cache still there, with no draw (by default, only when
it remembers at least what the site would give). That follows Bugnyar and Kotrschal (2002), whose
observers found every seen cache, and Heinrich and Pepper (1998), whose ravens found none of 40 they
hadn't seen and none of the seen ones after 14 days. A raid is Minds 6's pilfer: one take an
arrival, by `loot` and the carrying limit, and it replaces the tick's harvest. Watchers are dealt by
id as Minds 6's cheaters are, so at equal shares they are the same agents. `raid_when: hungry` puts
raiding on the owners' terms (only below R / 2). A survey-only probe lets a raid also harvest its
site. The page adds a Watching color mode, an inspector list of remembered caches, and charts of
pilfers by source, wasted raids and watchers against others.

Minds 8 ran twice on its branch before merging. The first design's survey was audited, three of its
five judged results turned out to be foreseeable, and a second round
(`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-01-minds-8-second-round-design.md`) replaced them with tests that
can tell the hypotheses apart. The second round's results are the milestone's results. The first
design's stay on record below as what it measured.

**The first design** (superseded; survey of 20 seeds, ticks 1–200, under the first round's raid
rule, now `raid_if: always`, and judges committed before any run).

Four statements in the first design's write-up went beyond what it showed, and are withdrawn:

- **"Watching can't reach the field's pilferage rates."** It wasn't shown. The rate compared a field
  hazard on fresh caches with a stock ratio diluted by caches months old. On fresh caches the second
  round finds watching's hazard twice stumbling's (below).
- **"Pilferage is bounded by knowledge, not action."** It was the reverse. Watchers saw 88 % of
  burials, but raids per burial were about 0.30 (the design audit). By the second round's rule the
  pilferage is action-bound in every world (below). The low share of caches known came mostly from
  old stock that nobody had seen recently.
- **"No frequency dependence" for watchers who bury.** It was not detected, which is weaker.
- **"Pure scroungers did worse at every share," read as a fact about scrounging.** Their shortfall
  predates watching: with watching off, the same agents, who never cache, survive 48 % against the
  hoarders' 70 % (with watching, 49 % against 65 %; `minds8b-presets.md`). It describes agents who
  never cache, not scroungers.

What the first design did isolate still stands, as measured under `raid_if: always`. Watching kept
theft's summer cost and dropped its winter pooling. Turning stumbling on at tick 100 in
`watch-winter` lifted survival per founder from 52.6 % to 81.7 %, and turning it off at tick 100 in
`theft-winter` dropped it from 85.7 % to 58.6 %, below no theft (70.0 %). The probe that lets a raid
also harvest recovered part of the summer gathering a raid replaces (34 800 a seed against 29 700,
and 42 300 without theft). The README's Minds 8 section summarizes the first design, and its spec's
amendments keep the full record, with the superseded statements marked.

**The second round** (survey in `survey/src/claims/minds8b.rs`; full results in
`survey/out/minds8b-results.md`). The new switches, all in `watching`:

- `raid_if: better | always` (default `better`, live): raid only when the remembered amount there is
  at least the site's value as rule M counts it; otherwise harvest and keep the memory.
- `value: amount | room` (live): a seen cache valued at the amount remembered, or capped at the room
  left under the carrying limit.
- `who: share | hoarders | cheaters` (reset-only): which founders watch.
- `scrounge: harvest | forgo` (live): a scrounger (an agent who watches and never caches) holding a
  fresh memory harvests as usual, or forgoes producing. Under `forgo` its only choices are its seen
  caches and staying put, and it harvests nothing on any tick it doesn't raid. This is Barnard and
  Sibly's assumption that scrounging excludes producing, in Vickery et al.'s (1991, p. 848) words.

Also new, in `caching`: `dig_below: half | reserve` (default `half`, live), because the calibrated
world for claim 2 needed owners who dig below their whole reserve and a survey probe can't be a
preset. Two presets are new: `watch-scroungers-forgo` (half scroungers who forgo, `loot: eat` so
that loot never fills a pack) and `watch-ak` (the calibrated world, everyone watching).

The second round wrote a pre-mortem before any judge: for each judge, what each hypothesis predicts,
checked against the numbers already seen. Fitness changed to a continuous measure: agent-ticks alive
per founder over ticks 1–200, divided by 200 (field worlds). Seeds 1–20 for claims 1 and 2, and 1–60
for claim 3. Span 7 is judged, and spans 1, 2, 3, 7 and 13 are reported. Thresholds are 80 % of
seeds. The judges were committed alone (e4edce3), with one clarification before any run (55a8c23: a
3a slope whose 95 % CI lies wholly above 0 reads Fails, "rising"). The calibration's rule was
committed before it ran, and its result before claims 2a–2d ran.

Usage is now a check, not a claim: raids take sugar in 20 of 20 seeds of every watching preset.

- **1. Fresh caches.** The cohort is every (site, owner) cache first created in ticks 1–90, followed
  for 7 ticks. In `watch-winter`, 6.6 % are taken within a day, 21 % within 3 days and 36 % within a
  week (h 6.1 % a day). With stumbling alone (`theft-winter`) it is 0.8 %, 5.0 % and 19 % (3.0 %),
  and with both (`watch-winter-stumble`) 7.8 %, 28 % and 50 % (9.6 %). For context, not as targets:
  Heinrich and Pepper's within-species next-day figure is at most 15 of 42 caches (36 %), against
  watching's 6.6 %. Vander Wall and Jenkins's band is cited for what it measures. The first design's
  stock rate, 0.70 % a tick under `better` (0.95 % under the first design's `always`), is kept as
  context.
- **The bottleneck: action-bound.** The rule, fixed before the run, compares the medians of the
  per-seed P(seen) (burials seen ÷ burials) and P(raid | seen) (caches seen that are raided within
  the span ÷ caches seen). Knowledge-bound iff P(seen) < P(raid | seen). In `watch-winter` they are
  0.883 and 0.541, so it is action-bound. It is action-bound in every watching world and setting
  reported, and in every seed: P(seen) 0.85–0.90 against P(raid | seen) 0.10–0.63, which rises with
  the span (0.105, 0.267, 0.368, 0.541 and 0.625 at span 1, 2, 3, 7 and 13).
- **2. Andersson and Krebs.** Watching flips the condition: with everyone watching, owners dig back
  39 % of their ended caches' sugar and thieves take 59 %, where without watching it was 78 % and
  22 %. But fitness hardly follows. The hoarders' fitness falls from 0.933 to 0.916 and the
  cheaters' rises from 0.938 to 0.943, so the hoarders' advantage falls by 0.023 (paired, in 20 of
  20 seeds). Across watching off and spans 1, 3, 7 and 13, its sign matches the condition's in 9,
  10, 12, 15 and 16 of 20 runs. Survival moves more than fitness: 67 % of the hoarders survive the
  first winter against 76 % of the cheaters, where without watching it is 87 % against 75 %. The
  factorial (`who`) splits the cost, and neither part reaches 0.05 in any seed. Watching only by the
  cheaters costs the hoarders 0.012 of advantage, in 18 of 20 seeds. Watching only by the hoarders
  changes their own fitness by no detectable amount.
- **3. Producers and scroungers.** Where scrounging excludes producing (`watch-scroungers-forgo`),
  the scroungers trail, and trail further as they become common. The median shortfall is 0.042 at a
  share of 0.1, rises to 0.228 at 0.8, and is 0.169 at 0.9. That is the negative frequency
  dependence Barnard and Sibly's game predicts. But no stable mix forms, because they trail even
  when rare. Only 18 of 60 seeds show any crossing from ahead to behind. Part of the shortfall when
  rare is likely the cost of never caching in a winter world, not of scrounging: in the same world
  with watching off, the same agents' fitness is 0.888 against the hoarders' 0.911 (48 % against
  70 % surviving the first winter), a gap of 0.023, about half the median 0.042 at a share of 0.1.
  The verdict is unchanged. Forgoing is the cost of scrounging:
  `scrounge: harvest` raises their fitness by 0.129 (95 % interval 0.114 to 0.145, in 20 of 20
  seeds), above the hoarders' (0.916 against 0.887). Among watchers who also bury
  (`watch-scroungers`), the median watcher is slightly ahead at every share, and no fall in that
  lead was detected. Meanwhile watching lowers the world's fitness (slope −0.008 per unit share), a
  mild social dilemma. Both hold only with a memory of 7 or 13 ticks; at spans 1, 2 and 3 the lead
  isn't detected (pooled 0.0007, −0.0015 and −0.0027).

With a memory of a day or two, watching takes fresh caches more slowly than stumbling, doesn't flip

**The probe** (reported for claims 1 and 2). With a raid also harvesting its site, `watch-winter`'s
hazard rises to 7.0 % a day and fitness to 0.911 (0.891 as set). In `watch-ak` the condition still
flips in 20 of 20 seeds (p_s ÷ p_o 0.560).

**An upper bound.** The mechanism is generous to watchers. Their attention is perfect: every burial
in sight is seen, at any distance up to vision. Their memory is exact for the whole span, and a span
of 7 is at the generous end of what's known. Cachers never hide, and owners never defend (both are
P3). In Bugnyar and Kotrschal's aviary, an individual observer was present at the final caching
event in only 23–60 % of trials (p. 188), and cachers hid about 80 % of their caches behind objects
that blocked the view (p. 191, Fig. 5). So read every second-round result as the most watching could
do in this world. Where even that is small (2b–2d), the result should survive a less generous
mechanism. Where it is large (1a, 2a, and 3a for scroungers who forgo), it depends on the span, and
at spans 1–2 it reverses. 3c's social dilemma depends on it too: the burying watchers' lead isn't
detected at spans 1, 2 and 3 (pooled 0.0007, −0.0015 and −0.0027) and holds only at 7 and 13.

**The lesson carried forward: a pre-registered judge can still be a foregone conclusion.** The first
design committed every judge before running, and three of its five results could have been read off
Minds 6's numbers: a band the metric couldn't reach, a ratio already at its floor, and "scroungers"
who still produced. Committing judges first guards against tuning, not against tests that can't
discriminate. The second round added a pre-mortem: for each judge, what each hypothesis predicts and
whether the number has already been seen, plus a check of floors, ceilings and caps. It found what
the first design couldn't. Watching flips Andersson and Krebs's condition but fitness doesn't
follow, scrounging that excludes producing is frequency-dependent without a stable mix, and the
results hang on the span. What the pre-mortem still missed was the scale. In ticks alive ÷ 200,
which sits near 0.9, 2c's and 2d's 0.05 per seed was a large effect, and their verdicts read as
"well under 0.05", not "no cost". The next pre-mortem should ask what a threshold means on the
measure's own scale, and say which unstated mechanism choices (here, raiding on every arrival) could
carry the result.

**Minds 9: a spatial P1b.** Minds 7's heritable larder probability and defense, in our Sugarscape
world with Minds 6's theft and Minds 8's watching. The spec will design it; given Minds 8's second
round, it should test:

- **Whether Vander Wall and Jenkins's threshold appears when apparency comes from mechanisms.**
  Minds 7 set how easily scattered caches and larders are found as parameters. In the world, a cache
  is found by stumbling (`find`) or by being seen buried, and the two differ in kind. With a 7-tick
  memory, watching takes fresh caches twice as fast as stumbling (6.1 % a day against 3.0 %), but
  only caches seen buried within the span. Stumbling reaches old caches all year. With a memory of a
  tick or two, watching is the slower of the two.
- **Whether a defended larder pays against watchers.** In `watch-ak`, watching flips Andersson and
  Krebs's condition, but the hoarders' fitness barely follows: it falls by 0.023, the sign of their
  advantage matches the condition's in only 62 of 100 runs, and neither being watched nor their own
  raiding costs them 0.05 in any seed. That is under a mechanism generous to watchers. A larder
  concentrates what can be seen in one defended place, so Minds 9 should ask whether defense earns
  anything when being watched costs this little, and report survival beside the compressed fitness
  measure.
- **Whether a mix of scroungers and producers forms once strategies are heritable.** At fixed
  shares, scroungers who forgo producing fall further behind as they become common but trail even
  when rare, so no stable mix formed (about half of that shortfall at a share of 0.1 is there with
  watching off, likely the cost of never caching in a winter world). With a memory of 7 or 13 ticks,
  watchers who also bury hold a small lead while the world's fitness falls with their share, a mild
  social dilemma; at spans 1–3 no lead is detected. At long spans, selection would be expected to
  remove forgoing scroungers and spread watching among burying agents at the world's expense. Minds
  9 should test both, and at spans 1–2, where the forgo result reverses and the dilemma goes
  undetected. Minds 6's open stability questions (the crossings at `find` 0.02 and 0.05, and whether
  reciprocity sustains hoarding once strategies can change) belong here too.
- **Whether hoarding survives what it displaces.** In the world a raid replaces a tick's harvest,
  and walking to caches spends moves. In `watch-winter`, about two thirds of watching's survival
  cost is the replaced harvest (the probe), and raiding on every arrival (`raid_if: always`) costs 6
  more points. These costs are part of what selection would weigh, and any result should report them
  beside the outcome.
- **Whether the results survive a less generous watcher.** Every Minds 8 result is an upper bound:
  perfect attention, exact memory, no hiding and no defense. Minds 9 should report its main results
  at a short span, and P3 (protection) then takes away the cachers' half of that generosity.
